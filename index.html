SpecStep

Verifiable intermediate rewards for LLM structure elucidation from literature NMR, IR and HRMS data

Vedant Nayak

The preprint is in preparation. Code, data and the RL environment will be released with it. How to cite

Draft. A chemist has not yet audited the answer key, and the numbers on this page may change.

Abstract

Benchmarks for structure elucidation from spectra score only the final molecule, which tells us little about which parts of a wrong answer are right and gives reinforcement learning (RL) a sparse signal. We introduce SpecStep, a dataset and RL environment built from 19,321 compounds whose ¹H NMR, ¹³C NMR, IR and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) data were reported in open-access articles. The molecular formula is withheld. A model must return its final structure together with five properties of it (formula, double-bond equivalents, functional groups, proton environments with neighbour counts, and carbon environments), and each is scored against the true structure with RDKit, with no human or LLM judge. We show that the formula is not given away by the mass alone: a median of 22 formulas fit each HRMS value within 5 ppm.

On a size-balanced 150-task core set, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra at maximum reasoning effort solve 75.3% and 69.3% of structures, a difference that is not statistically significant, ahead of GPT-6.1 Sol (60.7%), Grok 4.7 (36.7%) and the open-weight DeepSeek-V4-Pro (13.3%). Accuracy falls with molecular size: on 30 harder molecules of 36–45 heavy atoms, GPT-6 Astra solves the most (53%) and answers every task, while Opus 5.5 solves 43% and runs out of output budget on almost half. Much of the remaining gap is responses that do not finish rather than wrong chemistry. When frontier models get the structure right, their reported properties are almost always right too, whereas a weaker model’s properties often contradict its own answer. A sampling probe without training suggests the decomposed reward separates failed attempts that a final-answer reward scores identically. We release the grader, the environment, and train and test splits with per-task provenance and quality flags.

What is checked

The model reports six things in one answer. They follow the order a chemist works in, and each has a single correct value that RDKit computes from the true molecule.

  1. Molecular formula. Exact match. Only 4% of test tasks have a single formula that fits the mass within 5 ppm, so the model has to use the NMR counts too.
  2. Double-bond equivalents. Exact match.
  3. Functional groups. F1 over 23 groups defined by SMARTS patterns. A group that appears under one reading of aromaticity but not the other is optional and is neither credited nor penalised.
  4. Proton environments. F1 over sets of equivalent carbon-bound protons, each with its size and the number of protons on neighbouring carbons. Resolved diastereotopic and =CH₂ protons are accepted as separate sets.
  5. Carbon environments. The number of symmetry-distinct carbons. Full credit for an exact match, half if it is off by one.
  6. Structure. Connectivity match on the InChIKey first block, with tautomers accepted. A wrong structure earns half its Tanimoto similarity, so an answer with five right properties and a wrong structure can earn at most 0.725.

The structure carries 0.55 of the reward. Stereochemistry is not graded.

A denser signal for RL

Group-based RL methods such as GRPO learn nothing from a prompt when every sample in the group gets the same reward. We drew 8 samples on each of 40 training tasks from GPT-6.1 Sol at low effort, without any training, and scored the same samples three ways.

RewardGroups with no signalAll-wrong groups that still get a signal
Final answer only65%0 of 13
Format, valid SMILES and similarity40%11 of 13
SpecStep30%12 of 13

In every group with both right and wrong samples, SpecStep scored each right sample above each wrong one. This is a probe of the reward, not a training result.

When the model and the answer key disagree

Preliminary. These readings are ours and are waiting for review by a chemist.

On 300 test tasks (the core set and 150 more), Opus 5.5 gave a complete answer for 233 compounds, and 223 of those matched the record. We went back to the source papers for the other 10. In 7 of them the record itself looks wrong: the database converted a name to the wrong structure, or the paper’s name contradicts its own spectra. If that holds up, the headline accuracy is understated, and the screens we use to flag bad records need to be stricter.

The database got the structure wrong 2

  • 00026 Ketone recorded as an aldehyde

    The database has a biaryl aldehyde; the paper names a diaryl ketone.

    The paper's name matches the model, so the error came in when the database converted the name to a structure. The data fit the ketone: no CHO proton, C=O at 193.8 ppm, IR 1659 cm⁻¹, and a 1,2,4-substituted ring (7.56 d, 2.0 Hz; 7.19 dd, 8.4/2.0 Hz; 6.82 d, 8.4 Hz). Also, ‘3.92 (d, J = 1.8 Hz, 6H)’ is probably the two OMe singlets written as a doublet.

  • 00218 Pyridyl position not given in the paper

    The paper says only '7-pyridyl'; the database chose pyridin-2-yl; the data look like pyridin-3-yl.

    The name doesn't say where the pyridine is attached, and the database picked the 2-position. The signals at 8.63 (d, 1.9 Hz), 8.58 (dd, 4.8/1.7 Hz), 7.68 (dt, 7.8/2.0 Hz) and 7.31 (ddd, 7.8/4.8/0.9 Hz) look like a 3-substituted pyridine to me. The 8.63 doublet with only a 1.9 Hz coupling would be H-2, and a 2-substituted pyridine has no proton like that.

The paper’s name and its own data disagree 4

  • 00105 NMR and HRMS disagree

    The name and HRMS say 3,4-dimethylphenyl; the NMR shows two OMe groups.

    The ¹H list has two 3H singlets at 3.90 and 3.87 with carbons at 56.1/56.08, and two oxygenated aromatic carbons (149.5, 149.0). There is no aryl CH₃ near 2.2 ppm. That looks like 3,4-dimethoxyphenyl. The name and HRMS fit the dimethyl compound, so one part was probably copied from an analogue. The model went with the NMR and said so in its answer:

  • 00086 Named meta, data look para

    Named an ethyl 3-substituted benzoate; two 2H doublets and the authors' own '2xCH' labels suggest para.

    The ¹H list has two 2H doublets (7.97 and 7.69, J = 8.0 Hz), and in the paper the authors label the ¹³C signals at 130.5 and 120.0 as ‘2×CH’. There are 9 carbon signals; para gives 9 and meta would give 11. The ethyl quartet and triplet are also given J = 8.0 Hz, which looks like a typo.

  • 00025 Named meta, data look para (2)

    Named a 3-substituted phenyl sulfonate; a 2H doublet at 7.16 and 13 carbon signals suggest para.

    There is a 2H doublet at 7.16 (J = 7.9 Hz), which fits the two protons next to the sulfonate oxygen on a para ring. There are 13 carbon signals; para gives 13 and meta would need 15. Same pattern as case 6.

  • 00280 Linear or angular fusion?

    Named benzo[g] (linear); a deshielded 1H at 8.6 and an 8.6 Hz doublet, with no aromatic singlets, look angular.

    Linear fusion should give two aromatic singlets. Instead there is a 1H multiplet at 8.55–8.66 and a doublet at 7.81 (J = 8.6 Hz), and no singlets, which looks more like the angular isomer. I'm surer about the data than about the ring nomenclature.

The paper may have assigned the wrong product 1

  • 00070 C- or O-ethylation?

    Named a 3-ethyl chroman-4-one; the data may fit the 4-ethoxy-2H-chromene better.

    Only three sp³ carbons are listed (77.5, 70.8, 15.1). The chromanone needs four (C-2, a quaternary C-3, the CH₂ and the CH₃); the enol ether needs three. The ethyl CH₂ is at 3.62–3.79 ppm with its carbon at 70.8, where I'd expect an OCH₂, not a C-ethyl. Only one ketone carbon is listed (194.5), and the chromanone would need two. So I read this as O-alkylation of the 1,3-dicarbonyl, which would mean the paper misassigned the product. This is the case I'm least sure about.

The model is probably wrong 3

  • 00104 Pyrazolo[1,5-a]pyrimidine regiochemistry

    The paper and the record agree (5-amino-7-phenyl); the model proposed 7-amino-5-phenyl.

    The record matches the paper here. I don't think 1D ¹H/¹³C peak lists can tell these two regioisomers apart, so either the model is wrong or the question can't be answered from the data it was given.

  • 00181 Side-chain position

    The paper and the record agree (side chain at C6); the model put it at C5.

    The record matches the paper. H-6a is a doublet (J = 7.7 Hz), apparently coupled only to H-3a, which fits a quaternary C6 carrying the side chain. I think the model is wrong here.

  • 00094 1- or 2-naphthyl?

    The paper and the record agree (naphthalen-1-yl); the model proposed naphthalen-2-yl.

    The record matches the paper. The aromatic signals are given as ranges (7.53–7.56, 2H; 7.84–7.93, 4H; 8.30–8.34, 2H including isoxazole H-3), and I can't tell which isomer they fit better.

Use the environment

SpecStep ships as an environment for the verifiers framework. The reward can be swapped for the final-answer or similarity baselines, so you can compare them on the same tasks.

import verifiers as vf

env = vf.load_environment("specstep-env", reward="specstep")
# reward="final_only" or reward="tool_hygiene" for the baselines

Available with the preprint.

Data

Tasks come from NMRBank, which extracted NMR records from open-access articles in PubMed Central. We parsed the IR and HRMS values from the same paragraphs, removed the authors’ assignment labels, and kept a record only if its HRMS, ¹H integration and ¹³C peak count agree with the structure. Of 91,707 records, 19,321 passed: 15,107 for training, 50 for development and 350 for testing. Every task keeps its source article and licence, and only CC BY and CC0 articles are used.
